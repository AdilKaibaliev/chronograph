# Africa, 1300–1453: research and implementation record

Prepared 24 September 2026 for the local late-medieval extension. No deployment was performed by this subtask.

The implementation is `scripts/world/late-africa.cjs`. It uses the shared late-period helpers and preserves the earlier catalogue. All additions have Russian, English and Kyrgyz text. Research URLs are kept in the build-time source register; the website displays publication titles.

## Coverage

- 31 African profiles receive a phase in the new period, including 11 new profiles.
- 48 phases, 60 area frames and 14 dated or approximately dated events.
- The new profiles are Timbuktu, the continuing town of Djenné, Bornu, the Mamluk Sultanate, Ifat, Adal, Mogadishu, Mwenemutapa, Khami, Kongo and Portuguese Ceuta.
- Existing Mali, Gao, Kanem, Dongola, Alwa, Ethiopia, Lalibela, Kilwa, Gedi, Madagascar, Zimbabwe, Ife, Benin, Cairo, Kairouan, Marrakesh, Hafsid, Zayyanid and Marinid histories continue.
- Mapungubwe and earlier extinct dynasties are not extended merely to fill the map. Their successor centres are represented separately.

## Evidence and editorial decisions

### Western Sudan

[The Met, Sahel: Art and Empires](https://www.metmuseum.org/es/exhibitions/sahel-art-empire-sahara/inside-the-exhibition) supports Mali’s fourteenth-century prominence and Musa’s 1324 pilgrimage. [UNESCO, Timbuktu](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/119/) supports its mosque construction and scholarly role. [Ibn Battuta’s Travels, Fordham extracts](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/source/1354-ibnbattuta.asp) provides a contemporary account of the 1352–1353 journey.

The large Mali outline is marked **influence**, not a surveyed frontier. Gao’s independence is represented as a broad transition around 1400; the empire of Sonni Ali is explicitly not projected back from 1464. The visit to Mali locates Manden, not an asserted archaeological identification of the capital.

[Rice University, recent excavations at Jenné-jeno and Djenné](https://anthropology.rice.edu/sites/g/files/bxs3341/files/inline-files/djennemuseumsite.pdf) distinguishes the two settlements. The old Djenné-Djeno profile ends approximately in 1400. A separate Djenné profile continues thereafter. Starting that profile at 1300 is the extension window, not a claimed founding date.

### Lake Chad and the Nile

[Dierk Lange, L’Eviction des Sēfuwa du Kānem et l’origine des Bulāla](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-african-history/article/abs/leviction-des-sefuwa-du-kanem-et-lorigine-des-bulala/622B2FD9FFD57C38B1C12ABCB64818D1) places the decisive withdrawal in the reign of Umar, approximately 1382–1387, and stresses earlier Sefuwa connections with Bornu. The map uses **c. 1385**, not an exact invented day or a claim that Bornu was founded then. Kanem remains visible under the Bulala while Bornu becomes a separate dynastic centre.

[University of Warsaw, Dongola](https://pcma.uw.edu.pl/en/2019/04/02/dongola-2/) supplies the 1317 throne-hall mosque and 1364 court departure. These changes do not remove the local population or imply immediate universal conversion. Alwa continues in the southern Nile region; its outline is classified as influence because the late political geography is less well established.

### Egypt and al-Sham

[The Met, The Art of the Mamluk Period](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-art-of-the-mamluk-period-1250-1517) supports the Bahri/Burji division, military elite, patronage, commerce and the disruption caused by Timur. [Gohar Grigoryan Savary, Armenian Colophons on the Takeover of Sis](https://sonar.ch/documents/324316/files/armenian_colophons_on_the_takeover_of_si.pdf) supports the 1375 fall of Sis.

One exterior polygon joins Egypt and al-Sham; Cilicia is added in 1375. Timur’s campaign is shown as a located event in Damascus rather than falsely erasing the whole of al-Sham for two entire annual snapshots. The Hijaz is handled separately by the Eurasian module because Mamluk suzerainty and local sharifian government should not be conflated with Egyptian provincial administration. The Cairo city profile remains separate from the state profile.

### Horn of Africa

[Timothy Insoll, medieval Ethiopian archaeology](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/archaeology-of-complexity-and-cosmopolitanism-in-medieval-ethiopia-an-introduction/4F3CD1227F4A53E7732F4DAD9BE21F30) is used for diverse societies, trade and the Ifat/Walasma setting. [Bertrand Hirsch’s analysis of the Amda Seyon war narrative](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/348879933_Le_recit_des_guerres_du_roi_Amda_Seyon_contre_les_sultanats_islamiques_fiction_epique_du_xve_siecle) cautions against treating a later literary narrative as a precise eyewitness itinerary.

The module shows broad regional change under Amda Seyon and Zara Yaqob and an approximate eastward Walasma transition from Ifat to Adal. The 1376 frame is a periodisation of that transition, not a claim that all lands changed hands on one day. No exact battlefield or march is invented for the disputed narrative. Christian and Muslim polities are separate contemporary societies, not a single religious colour covering the Horn.

### Coast, southern Africa and Madagascar

[UNESCO, Kilwa and Songo Mnara](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/144/), [UNESCO, Gedi](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1720/) and [Graham Connah, Indian Ocean networks](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/african-civilizations/indian-ocean-networks-the-east-african-coast-and-islands/90D2611A7386C1FCB020045EBB98B4D9) support the port histories. Coastal settlement symbols do not claim that Kilwa ruled the full eastern African hinterland.

[UNESCO, Great Zimbabwe](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/364/) and [UNESCO, Khami](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/365/) underpin the fifteenth-century shift of political centres. The Khami page’s introductory summary contains a sixteenth-century statement inconsistent with its own detailed statement of outstanding value; the latter and the Great Zimbabwe statement place the transition around 1450. This version uses **c. 1450**, retains the Zimbabwe settlement and does not describe a single sudden universal abandonment.

The initial Mwenemutapa frame is an approximate northern core around 1430, following the conventional early-fifteenth-century chronology in the African historical synthesis. It is not the much larger sixteenth-century empire and should be reviewed further before a specialised cartographic release.

[Burney, Finding the Connections](https://ethnobotanyjournal.org/index.php/era/article/download/94/81/334) and the existing Crowther archaeological source support Madagascar’s settlement, agriculture and oceanic connections. The island is not portrayed as one medieval kingdom or entirely uninhabited after a port’s decline.

### Guinea, Kongo and the Maghreb

[The Met, Ife Terracottas](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/ife-terracottas-1000-1400-a-d) and the existing Ife collection record support its artistic and sacred role. [Art Institute of Chicago, Ewuare](https://archive.artic.edu/benin/oba/) supports the approximately 1440 royal transition. Benin’s enlarged area is influence rather than the later empire’s full extent. [Royal Museum for Central Africa, The Kongo kingdom](https://www.afrikamuseum.be/en/discover/history_articles/kongo-kingdom) locates its formation at the end of the fourteenth century; **c. 1400** is a rounded display date.

[Museum With No Frontiers, Algeria timeline](https://islamicart.museumwnf.org/hcr.php?begin=0&country=dz&id=object%3BISL%3Buk%3BMus04%3B40%3Bar&start_date=1300), [Caferro and Allehbi on the Hafsid civil war](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1017/rqx.2025.23) and [Rosenthal’s introduction to Ibn Khaldun](https://ignca.gov.in/Asi_data/16173.pdf) inform the Marinid advances and reversals. Tlemcen and Tunis do not simultaneously remain independent sovereign areas during the displayed Marinid occupations. Adjacent polygons are joined into a single exterior outline when governed together, avoiding artificial internal borders.

[Museu de Marinha, Tomada de Ceuta](https://cultura.marinha.pt/pt/museumarinha_web/multimedia_web/Paginas/efemeride-tomada-de-ceuta-21ago20.aspx) supports the 1415 conquest. Only the foothold is marked; the map does not colour Morocco as Portuguese.

The source register also retains the established scholarly syntheses: *The Cambridge History of Africa*, Volume 3, and UNESCO’s *General History of Africa*, Volume IV. Their entire contents were not reread in this subtask. Specific accessible articles, institutional descriptions, source extracts and catalogues were checked for the decisions above.
